ACH

Analysis of Competing Hypotheses. Don't seek to confirm your favorite theory, actively try to disprove it. Test ALL hypotheses simultaneously and eliminate the weakest ones. Developed by the CIA to combat confirmation bias.

"The analyst must not seek to confirm the hypothesis, but to actively refute it."
🎯 Why ACH?

The Problem: Your brain naturally seeks evidence that confirms what you already believe (confirmation bias). You unconsciously ignore contradictory evidence and over-weight supporting evidence. This leads to overconfident, fragile conclusions.

The Solution: ACH flips the script. Instead of proving ONE hypothesis right, you generate MULTIPLE competing hypotheses and test each piece of evidence against ALL of them. Then you eliminate hypotheses with too many inconsistencies. The last one standing isn't "proven true", it's just the least refutable.

The Result: Transparent, auditable analysis. Decision-makers see exactly which hypotheses you considered, why you rejected them, and how confident you should be in your conclusion. No more black-box reasoning.

❌ Wrong Way vs ✅ Right Way
✗
Biased Approach
Hypothesis: "This Twitter account is a Russian bot"
  • Look for Russian language → Found!
  • Look for automated patterns → Found!
  • Conclusion: "Definitely Russian bot" (80% confident)
Problem: Never tested alternative explanations. Ignored contradictory evidence. Overconfident result.
✓
ACH Approach
Hypotheses: Test ALL possibilities simultaneously
  • H1: Russian state bot
  • H2: Chinese bot
  • H3: Legitimate automation tool
  • H4: Human using scheduling software
Compare each evidence against ALL four. Eliminate based on inconsistencies. Calibrated confidence based on evidence quality.
📊 The ACH Matrix
Each piece of evidence is tested against every hypothesis. Mark as Consistent (C), Inconsistent (I), or Not Applicable (N/A). The hypothesis with the fewest inconsistencies is the strongest candidate.

🔄 4 Core Steps
1
Generate ALL Hypotheses
Don't just pick your favorite. Brainstorm 3-7 mutually exclusive possibilities. Include "unlikely but not impossible" options. Use Starbursting or spectrum thinking (e.g., innocent → manipulated → coordinated → state-sponsored).
2
List ALL Evidence
Collect every piece of evidence—factual observations, metadata, behavioral patterns, absences. Rate each for reliability (source quality) and diagnosticity (how much it helps distinguish hypotheses). Include evidence that contradicts your initial hunch.
3
Build the Matrix
Create a grid: Evidence (rows) × Hypotheses (columns). For each cell, mark C (consistent), I (inconsistent), or N/A. Count inconsistencies per hypothesis. The one with the fewest "I"s is your strongest candidate—not "proven," just least refuted.
4
Test Sensitivity
Identify "linchpins"—critical evidence that, if removed or wrong, would change your conclusion. If 1-2 pieces of evidence drive your entire result, your confidence should be LOW. Robust conclusions depend on multiple independent sources.
